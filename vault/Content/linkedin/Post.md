In high school, I wrote a paper arguing that video games are legitimate works of art. 

I broke down how a game’s soundtrack, world development, and storytelling can put you right in a character’s shoes—making you feel their loss, their tragedy, and their victories. My English teacher was so captivated by the paper that he called me out in front of the whole class. He looked at everyone and said I was going to be a big-time game developer one day. I still remember the stares. Some people were impressed, but most of them looked at me like I was just a complete nerd and a total weirdo. For a long time after high school, I tried to hide that side of myself. I tried to fit in, act corporate, and speak the way I thought professionals were supposed to speak. It took me years to realize something critical:Trying to fit in or sound like a corporate script completely kills your impact. Real authority doesnt come from hiding your unique interests or trying to fit into a box—it comes from owning your authentic self, weirdness and all. The moment I stopped fearing judgment and brought my full, unedited honesty into conversations—whether it was in boardroom meetings, code reviews, or client demos—everything changed: Building deep, meaningful relationships became natural instead of forced. Technical discussions turned into genuine human trust. People actually listened, because I wasnt reciting a script—I was just being real. Your weirdness isnt a flaw.
